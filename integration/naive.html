12  Naive integration

Highlights

  • A review of the naive Calc I definition of the integral
  • Why basing a theory on the naive definition is insufficient.

12.1 Preparation

Most calculus students strongly associate integrals with taking antiderivatives, and for good reason. Typically, the most efficient way to calculate the exact value of a definite integral is to employ the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, which tells us how to use the antiderivative of the integrand. However, in order to understand how definite integrals can be applied in both abstract mathematics and in the real world, we should not lose sight of the definition of the definite integral in terms of Riemann sums.

We will start with a review of how the definite integral is presented in a typical Calc I course, what we’ll call the “naive integral”. However, as we shall see, this definition fails to produce a self-contained theory of integration that behaves nicely.

As a result, it will be impossible to prove the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus linking integration and differentiation using the naive definition of the integral. This serves to motivate the more robust definition of the Riemann integral in the next section. It will also be a reminder of what you know from calculus and a warmup for what’s to come.

Definition 12.1 Let \(a<b\), and suppose \(f\) is a real-valued function whose domain contains \([a,b].\) Define the right Riemann Sum of \(f\) on the interval \([a,b]\) with \(n\) subintervals to be \[ R_n(f)=\sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i)\Delta x \qquad \text{where $\Delta x=\frac{b-a}{n}$ and $x_i=a+i\Delta x$} \tag{12.1}\]

Exercise 12.2 Let \(f:[1,3]\rightarrow\R\) be defined by \(f(x)=\sqrt x.\) Use a calculator to find \(R_{10}(f).\)

Note that we aren’t initially making any assumptions about the function \(f\) being “nice” in any way. In particular, \(f\) might be badly discontinuous as in the next example.

Exercise 12.3 Let \(f:[0,1]\rightarrow\R\) be defined by \(f(x)=0\) when \(x\) is irrational and \(f(x)=1\) when \(x\) is rational. We call this function Dirichlet’s function. Sketch a graph of \(f\) (as best you can), and find a formula for \(R_n(f).\)

In calculus, we are taught to think of right Riemann sums as approximations to the exact value of the integral. Visually, for non-negative-valued functions at least, we can represent this by representing the products \(f(x_i)\Delta x\) as rectangles; as \(n\) tends to \(\infty,\) this set of rectangles seems to better and better approximate the region under the curve, as in the demo below that you are likely familiar with.

And thus we make the following definition.

Definition 12.4 Let \(a<b,\) and suppose \(f\) is a real-valued function whose domain contains \([a,b].\) If the sequence \((R_n(f))\) of right Riemann sums of \(f\) over \([a,b]\) converges to some real number \(I,\) we say that \(f\) is right-integrable on \([a,b].\) In this case, we write \({\mathsf R}_a^b f\) for \(I\) and call this value the right integral of \(f\) over \([a,b].\)

This definition does two things for us. First it tells us which functions get to be part of our theory—ones where the sequence of right Riemann sums converges. And second it tells us what the value of those integrals should be. While the second part is what we focus on when doing calculations, it’s the first part that’s important for being able to prove theorems; it lays out the baseline assumptions we can make about the functions being integrated.

The problem with this definition is that those baseline assumptions are too weak to be able to derive theorems from. Definition 12.4 allows too many problematic functions into the theory without being able to properly handle them. The next example hints at the sort of thing that can go wrong.

Exercise 12.5 Show that Dirichlet’s function from Exercise 12.3 is right-integrable, and find its right integral. Does the value of the right integral make sense given the sketch you made? Explain.

As one more warmup question to reflect on, the following exercise provides another hint about an arbitrary and possibly-problematic choice we make in the definitions so far.

Exercise 12.6 Why do you think we refer to the expression in Definition 12.1 as the “right” Riemann sum? What would it mean to take the “left” Riemann sum?

12.2 The Domain Additivity Issue

We haven’t proven it yet, but it should be true that, for any function that’s part of our theory, the integral from \(a\) to \(b\) plus the integral from \(b\) to \(c\) of a function should be equal to the integral from \(a\) to \(c.\) This theorem should make intuitive sense if we are thinking about areas under the curve, but it is also one of the keys to proving the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

Unfortunately, right-integrable functions do not necessarily obey the corresponding rule as you’ll show below.

Exercise 12.7 For Dirichlet’s function \(f\) defined in Exercise 12.3, show that \(f\) is right-integrable over \(\left[0,\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right]\) and over \(\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt2},1\right]\). Find \(\mathsf R_0^{1/\sqrt2} f\) and \(\mathsf R_{1/\sqrt2}^1 f,\) and show that \[ \mathsf R_0^{1/\sqrt2} f+\mathsf R_{1/\sqrt2}^1 f\neq \mathsf R_0^1 f. \]

Hint

In your answer to Exercise 12.7, you can feel free to use familiar facts about whether sums, products, etc. of rational and irrational numbers are rational or irrational without proof, but make sure your facts are actually true!

Because of this and related issues, we are forced to seek an alternative to what we’ll call the “naive theory of integration” that arises from Definition 12.4.

To gain a foothold on how to do this, we start by returning to Exercise 12.6, and trying to conceptualize what’s going on in the definition of right Riemann sums. When calculating \(R_n(f),\) we should imagine dividing the interval \([a,b]\) into \(n\) (equal) subintervals, \([x_0,x_1],[x_1,x_2],\ldots,[x_{n-1},x_n].\) Then each summand \(f(x_i)\Delta x\) of Equation 12.1 can be thought of as the width of the \(i\)th subinterval (always \(\Delta x\)) times the value of the function at a certain point in that subinterval; namely \(f(x_i)\) is the value of the function at the right endpoint of the \(i\)th interval \([x_{i-1},x_i].\)

However, the choice to plug in the right endpoint of each interval is somewhat arbitrary. For a well-behaved function, we would expect that choosing the left endpoints, midpoints, or any other sample point \(x_i^*\) from the \(i\)th subinterval would be just as reasonable, and that as long as the subintervals are all small enough, adding up the products of the values \(f(x_i^*)\) at these sample points with the widths \(\Delta x\) will get us close to the true value of the integral.

Exercise 12.8  

  1. Write down a reasonable definition for the left Riemann sum \(L_n(f),\) what it means for a function to be left-integrable, and the left integral \(\mathsf L_a^b f\) of a real-valued function \(f\) whose domain includes \([a,b].\)
  2. Prove that \(f\) is right-integrable on \([a,b]\) if and only if \(f\) is left-integrable on \([a,b]\) and that in this case, \(\mathsf R_a^b f=\mathsf L_a^b f.\)

Exercise 12.9 For Dirichlet’s function \(f\) defined in Exercise 12.3, prove that for any \(n\in\N,\) there exist sample points \(x_i^*\) in the \(i\)th subinterval such that \(\sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i^*)\Delta x=0.\)

The previous exercise shows that for Dirichlet’s function, the choice of sample points does matter. That dependence will be grounds for us to exclude this function from the more rigorous theory that we develop in the remainder of this chapter1.

Review Questions

  1. What does it mean for a function to be “right-integrable”? What is the definition of the “right integral” of such a function?
  2. Give an example of a familiar property of integrals that can fail if we adopt the naive theory of integration based on right integrability.
